Why This Guide Exists

The modular and prefab housing market is full of products that look similar but perform very differently. Marketing copy is optimized to convert, not to inform — and the result is buyers who research extensively and still end up with the wrong product. This guide cuts through the noise and gives you the framework to make a confident, informed decision the first time.

Start Here: Your Three Use Cases

Before evaluating any specific product, identify which use case describes you most accurately:

  • Use Case A — emergency response — prioritize rapid deployment time and weatherproofing over long-term durability; modular units beat traditional construction by weeks
  • Use Case B — long-term auxiliary structure — verify local zoning rules on permanent vs temporary designation before ordering
  • Use Case C — off-grid installation — factor in electrical, water, and waste management systems in the total project cost

Your use case determines which product attributes actually matter to your decision. Buyers who try to optimize for all three simultaneously often end up with an overspecified product that costs more than they need to spend, or an underspecified product that fails to deliver in the situation that matters most. Know your primary use case and optimize for it.

The Attributes That Actually Matter

Fitment and compatibility — your specific use case — emergency temporary shelter vs long-term modular vs permanent installation. This is non-negotiable; a product that doesn't fit your application correctly is worth nothing regardless of its other qualities.

Materials and construction — structural integrity — steel-framed with weatherproof insulated panels for year-round use; R-value ratings that match your climate. This is where the quality gap between budget, mid-range, and premium products is most visible and measurable.

Installation requirements — setup timeline — emergency shelters deploy in hours; modular units take days to weeks depending on complexity and site prep. A product that installs cleanly without special tools or professional labor costs less in total than one that requires both to be done correctly.

Warranty and support — structural warranty and compliance with local building codes; verify permit requirements before ordering. Evaluate warranty by actual claim experience, not by the language in the warranty terms. Reviews mentioning warranty outcomes tell you what you actually need to know.

Price vs total cost of ownership — $5,000–$75,000+ depending on size, permanence, and specification. Calculate cost over the expected use period. The sticker price is only part of the equation.

How to Read the Reviews

Most buyers rely too heavily on aggregate ratings and too little on the review text. Here's a more reliable approach:

Filter to verified purchases — always. Unverified reviews are easy to manufacture. Start there before reading anything.

Sort by most recent — products change. Manufacturing quality changes. A 4.8-star rating from 2022 doesn't predict 2025–2026 product quality.

Read 3-star reviews first — 3-star reviews are written by buyers who neither love nor hate the product enough to push to the extremes. They tend to be the most balanced and honest assessment of actual strengths and weaknesses.

Look for failure patterns in 1-star reviews — one or two 1-star reviews for bad luck; five or more citing the same specific failure is a design or quality control problem, not a fluke.

Discount short-text review clusters — a bunch of 5-star reviews with brief, similar text is a red flag for review manipulation. Look for reviews with specific, personal details about the buyer's use case.

The Price Tier Decision

The modular and prefab housing market breaks into three tiers, and choosing the right tier for your situation is often more important than choosing the right product within a tier.

Budget tier (bottom 20% of price range) — appropriate for very occasional use, low-stakes applications, or where the cost of failure is low. Usually involves compromises on materials quality, manufacturing precision, or warranty support that matter more in demanding use than casual use.

Mid-range tier — where most buyers find the best value. Sufficient quality for typical use cases at a price that doesn't require a significant commitment. The gap between mid-range and premium is often smaller than the price difference suggests for moderate-intensity applications.

Premium tier — justified for high-frequency use, demanding conditions, safety-critical applications, or situations where the cost of a product failure significantly exceeds the cost of the product. Premium brands invest in materials, manufacturing consistency, and warranty support that pays off under pressure.

Choose the tier that matches your actual use intensity — not the tier that matches your aspirational use intensity. Most buyers overestimate how demanding their use case is when they're making a purchase and underestimate it when they're deciding whether to upgrade after a budget product fails.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Vague fitment claims — "universal fit" or "fits most" without specific compatibility data suggests the manufacturer can't guarantee fit for your application
  • No identifiable manufacturer — products with no traceable brand, manufacturer address, or customer service contact have no accountability when something goes wrong
  • Suspiciously low pricing — significantly below market rate for a comparable product usually means something was cut: materials, testing, quality control, or all three
  • Missing certifications for safety-critical applications — for anything where failure has safety implications, relevant certifications should be easy to find and verify
  • Review patterns that don't feel organic — identical review text, sudden clusters of 5-star reviews, or unusually high review velocity for a new product all suggest manipulation

Do You Need a Permit for a Shed?

Permit requirements for sheds vary by municipality, but the general rule is: structures under 120 square feet often do not require a building permit in most US jurisdictions. This is why 10x12 and 12x10 shed sizes are so popular — they sit just under the threshold. However, this threshold varies: some jurisdictions allow up to 200 sq ft without a permit, others require permits for any permanent structure regardless of size. Always check your local zoning ordinance before building.

Even when a building permit is not required, other rules may apply. Setback requirements (minimum distance from property lines, fences, and the main structure) apply in almost every jurisdiction. HOA rules may be more restrictive than municipal zoning. Some areas require permits for any structure with a concrete foundation, even small ones. The safest approach is to call your local building department — most will tell you the rules over the phone in 10 minutes.

Wood vs Metal vs Resin Sheds

Wood sheds are the most customizable and have the best resale appeal, but require the most maintenance — painting, staining, and treating for rot every 3–5 years. Metal sheds are low-maintenance and strong but prone to condensation and rust in humid climates. Resin/plastic sheds require zero maintenance and never rust or rot, but look less attractive and provide less insulation than wood or metal.

Foundation Options

A proper foundation extends shed life dramatically. Options from cheapest to most permanent: gravel pad (good drainage, easy DIY), concrete deck blocks (fast, no permit usually required), pressure-treated skids (common for prefab sheds), and poured concrete slab (most permanent, may require permit). For most residential sheds, a gravel pad or concrete deck blocks on level ground is sufficient.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size shed can I build without a permit?

In most US jurisdictions, structures under 120 square feet do not require a building permit. However, this varies — always verify with your local building department. Setback requirements from property lines still apply even for permit-exempt structures.

How long do prefab sheds last?

A well-maintained wood shed lasts 20–30 years. Metal sheds 10–20 years in dry climates, less in humid ones. Resin/plastic sheds 10–20 years depending on UV exposure. Foundation quality is the biggest variable — a shed on a proper gravel or concrete base will outlast one on bare soil by decades.

Can I put a shed on gravel?

Yes — a 4-6 inch compacted gravel pad is one of the best foundations for a shed. It provides excellent drainage, prevents rot under wood floors, and is easy to level. Use crushed gravel (not pea gravel) and compact it thoroughly before placing the shed.
